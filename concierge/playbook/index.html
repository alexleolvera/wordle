01

What this job actually is

A member pays us so they never have to wonder who to call. Your job is to make that true. You are not a receptionist taking messages and you are not a contractor giving advice — you are the person who makes something happen and then makes sure it happened.

The one sentence that decides everything

If you are unsure what to do, ask: "what would make this member feel handled?" Usually the answer is a reply in under fifteen minutes that says what happens next and when — even when the answer is "I don't know yet, I'll know by four."

Hours

7 AM – 7 PM, seven days. Outside those hours, requests wait until morning — except the three emergencies in section 03, which you act on whenever you see them.

03

Triage — what kind of thing is this?

Sort every incoming request into one of four buckets before you do anything else.

Not our job — safety first

Smell of gas → tell them to leave the house now and call the gas company or 911 from outside. Fire, smoke, sparking panel → 911. Water actively flooding → tell them where the main shutoff usually is, then dispatch emergency plumbing.

You are never the first call for a genuine emergency. Say so plainly, then help with what comes after.

Urgent — today if humanly possible

No cooling in summer, no heat in a freeze, no power to a significant part of the house, an active leak that is contained, a lock-out, a fridge or freezer down with food in it. Push these to the front and tell the member you are doing so.

Normal — this week

Everything else that is broken. Slow drains, a dead outlet, a sticking door, an appliance misbehaving, water stains, gutters. Most of your work lives here.

Planned — whenever it fits

Pressure washing, gutter cleaning, a fan install, maintenance we reminded them about. Batch these; they are where you fill a contractor's slow week and earn goodwill.

05

Dispatch rules

  1. Check the bench first. Anyone whose insurance, licence or W-9 is expired does not get the job. No exceptions, no "just this once" — this is the rule that protects the whole business.
  2. Send to two, not one, for anything urgent. First to reply gets it; tell the other one straight away and thank them. Contractors forgive losing a race. They do not forgive being ignored.
  3. Give them 30 minutes during business hours. No reply, move to the next on the bench. Log the non-reply — that number decides who gets offers next month.
  4. Rotate within a trade until you have twenty jobs of history on each vendor. After that, favour the fast and the well-rated, but never let one vendor have everything: the day they are busy becomes your problem.
  5. Match the job to the trade honestly. A handyman is not an electrician. If you are unsure which trade it is, send a photo to the vendor you trust most and ask — they will tell you for free.
  6. Share access details only on the day. Gate codes and lockbox numbers go out the morning of the visit, never at booking.
06

Reviewing a quote

This is the single most visible thing a member is paying for. Give it five real minutes.

CheckWhat you are looking for
Scope matches the problemMember said "slow drain", quote says "repipe". That gap is the conversation.
Against past jobsTwo or three comparable jobs in the same trade. Note the numbers so the next person has comparables too.
Repair vs replaceIf a replacement is quoted, ask outright whether a repair would hold. Sometimes yes. Members remember when you ask.
Parts and labour splitNot always given, but ask when the number is large. A quote nobody can explain is a quote to question.
Over the owner's thresholdAnything above $500 (or whatever the owner has set) goes to them before it goes to the member.

How to push back on a contractor

Never haggle and never imply they are cheating. Ask a question instead: "This came in about 30% above the last two we've had done — is there something about this one I should understand?" Half the time there is a real reason and you have just learned something. The other half, the number comes down on its own.

07

When it goes wrong

The contractor no-shows

Call the contractor first, then the member — in that order, so you have an answer before you make the call. Apologise without excuses, give a new time, and log it. Two no-shows and that vendor comes off the bench.

The member is unhappy with the work

Get specifics and photos. Go back to the contractor and ask them to make it right — every vendor signed a re-service policy, so this is a reminder, not a favour. Keep the member updated even when there is nothing new.

If the contractor refuses, stop and escalate. Do not promise a refund, a credit or a replacement contractor on your own authority.

A contractor wants to pay us for the job

They cannot, and neither can the member pay us for the work. The member pays the contractor directly. We invoice the contractor separately for the network fee. If anyone tries to route repair money through us, say no and tell the owner.

Nobody in the trade will take it

Tell the member honestly and early. Offer what you can — a name outside the network, or a date further out. A member who is told the truth stays. A member who is strung along for a week does not.

09

The routine

Every morning, before anything else

Read overnight messages. Reply to all of them, even the ones you cannot solve yet. Check today's scheduled jobs and confirm each contractor is still coming.

Through the day

Triage as things arrive. Chase any quote older than eighteen hours. Chase any offer that has been out over thirty minutes. Log every job as it moves — a job that is not logged did not happen, and the fee on it will never be collected.

End of day

Nothing unanswered. Tomorrow's visits confirmed. Yesterday's completed jobs followed up. If something is stuck, note it so the morning starts with it.

Every Friday

Check which vendor documents expire in the next 30 days and chase them. Note any vendor who missed the response target twice this week. Send the owner: jobs done, median match time, anything that went wrong, anything that nearly did.

10

Never, under any circumstances

  • Never quote a price yourself. Not even a ballpark, not even when pushed. Contractors quote; we review. A number from you becomes a promise.
  • Never send a vendor whose insurance, licence or W-9 has lapsed. Not for a small job, not for a regular, not just this once.
  • Never take payment for the repair work. The member pays the contractor. We are not in that transaction and the business depends on staying out of it.
  • Never give out access details before the day of the visit. Gate codes, lockbox numbers, alarm codes — morning of, to the assigned contractor, nobody else.
  • Never promise a time you have not confirmed with the contractor. "I'll find out and come straight back" is always available and always better.
  • Never go quiet on a member. If you have no news, send the no-news text. Silence is the only unrecoverable mistake on this list.
  • Never diagnose. You are not a plumber. "I don't know, but I know who will" is the right answer and members respect it.

This is an operating document, not a contract. Thresholds — the quote limit, the hours, the response targets — are set by the owner and may differ from the defaults here. Confirm them on day one and write the real numbers in.

Nothing here is legal advice. The rules about who pays whom, and about not quoting work, exist because the business depends on the platform not being the contractor. Do not work around them to solve a problem in the moment.
